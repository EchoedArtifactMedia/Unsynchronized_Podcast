Planning & Scheduling

Every production cycle begins the same way: by clearing my dry erase calendar and starting with a blank slate. Once the previous month's deadlines have been erased, I map out the new production cycle by adding episode release dates, research deadlines, website updates, transcript work, and other milestones that need to be completed before publication. Having a visual overview of the entire process helps me keep track of multiple moving pieces and ensures important dates don't slip through the cracks.

With the schedule established, I move into outlining and research. At this stage, I review the materials I've already collected, identify gaps that still need to be filled, and begin organizing information for the next episode. While much of this work happens digitally through spreadsheets, bookmark folders, research documents, and archived sources, I still rely heavily on physical tools such as sticky notes, notepads, pens, pencils, and push pins to organize ideas, sketch rough outlines, and keep track of details throughout the drafting process.

Social media planning happens much later in the workflow. Once an episode is nearly complete and publication is approaching, I begin preparing promotional content and scheduling future posts. While I no longer use the Metricool Social Media Scheduler as part of my current process, I found it to be a straightforward and user-friendly platform for scheduling social media content across multiple accounts and would still recommend it to creators looking for a centralized scheduling solution.

Tools used: Dry erase calendar*, dry erase markers*, eraser for whiteboard*, sticky notes*, note pads*, ballpoint pens*, and push pins*.

Storage & Organization

Almost every aspect of Unsynchronized ultimately ends up on my external hard drive. Scripts, archived webpages, transcripts, spreadsheets, audio recordings, graphics, and episode backups are all stored there. As the project has grown, reliable storage has become increasingly important because each episode generates a large collection of supporting materials that need to remain accessible long after an episode is released. Maintaining a separate backup location also helps protect against data loss and makes it easier to keep years of research organized and available for future projects.

Tools used: Western Digital 2 TB SSD*

Recording & Production

Once a script is finalized, production shifts from planning and research to recording and editing. All of the audio content I record for Unsynchronized is made using a basic microphone mounted on a boom arm with a pop filter to reduce unwanted noise and improve vocal clarity. I also use monitoring headphones, which allow me to review audio more closely than regular headphones or earbuds.

I record audio with Audacity, which remains one of the most accessible and capable free audio recording programs available. After recording, I use Auphonic to process and enhance the audio before preparing it for release. Once everything is complete, RSS.com serves as my podcast host and distributes new episodes to podcast directories and listening platforms.

Many of the tools used during production aren't podcast-specific, but they make the workflow significantly easier. My USB hub helps manage multiple peripherals I need to connect to my computer, while my laptop stand, wired mouse, and ergonomic mousepad make working more comfortable and overall.

Tools used: Fifine USB/XLR microphone*, Fifine monitoring headphones*, microphone boom arm*, microphone pop filter*, Audacity audio recording software*, Auphonic audio editing software*, RSS.com podcast hosting*, Acer USB hub*, Lenovo wired mouse*, laptop stand, and ergonomic mousepad.

Graphics & Creative Resources

Every episode of Unsynchronized requires visual assets, whether that's episode art or promotional content for social media. Most image editing is performed in the Gnu Image Manipulation Program (GIMP), which remains my preferred graphics editor, and the G'MIC plug-in expands the range of filters and effects I can use for creating my visual content.

For video production, I primarily assemble my videos in ClipChamp, a video editor that's created my Microsoft and integrated to newer versions of Windows OS. When I need capabilities that ClipChamp doesn't easily provide, I usually turn to Canva. In particular, Canva has been useful for creating animated captions and stylized transcript videos that synchronize on-screen text with spoken audio. To support artwork and visual design, I also make frequent use of free stock photography resources such as Unsplash, Pexels, and Pixabay.

Tools used: GIMP, G'MIC QT plug-in, Canva, Unsplash, Pexels, and Pixabay.
